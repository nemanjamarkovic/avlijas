Documents for repatriation to Serbia, Bosnia, Montenegro and North Macedonia

Moving human remains across a border always takes two kinds of paperwork: documents from the country where the person died and a permit from the country where the funeral will be. Below you will find what each of 15 European countries requires, when a multilingual certificate is enough and when an apostille is needed, and what the Serbian permit (sprovodnica) involves. We obtain all of it for the family.

How the documents fit together

The first group proves the death and allows the remains to leave the country of death: a medical certificate of death and its cause, the civil death certificate from the local registry, and a permit to take the remains abroad. The second group is the destination country's permit to let the remains in — for Serbia, the sprovodnica.

On top of that, the original passport or ID card of the deceased is required. All papers travel with the coffin and are shown at the border, so they must be complete before departure. Names and issuing authorities change from country to country; the structure does not.

International death certificate or apostille and translation

What is an international death certificate? It is the multilingual extract from the death register defined by CIEC Convention No. 16 (Vienna, 1976). When both the issuing country and Serbia apply the convention, the extract is generally accepted without legalisation and without translation.

The convention is in force for Serbia, Bosnia and Herzegovina, Montenegro and North Macedonia and, among the countries on this page, for Germany, Austria, Switzerland, Italy, Spain, France, Croatia, Slovenia, the Netherlands and Belgium.

Greece signed the convention but never ratified it, and Sweden, Denmark and Norway are not parties. Documents from these four countries need an apostille in the country of issue and a translation into Serbian by an authorised translator. Since this adds time, we start on both as soon as the document is issued.

Germany, Austria and Switzerland

In the three German-speaking countries the documents have similar names, but the offices that issue them differ — and in Germany even the rules for urns depend on the state. Berlin, for instance, requires a Leichenpass for a coffin but not for an urn, and the crematorium releases an urn for abroad only once it has confirmation of the intended burial.

  • Germany: medical death certificate (Todesbescheinigung, also Totenschein), civil death certificate (Sterbeurkunde) from the registry office (Standesamt) and the Leichenpass. The office issuing the Leichenpass depends on the federal state — in Bavaria the municipality where the transport begins, in Thuringia the health office of the place of death. An unnatural death first needs the prosecutor's release (Freigabe).
  • Austria: coroner's report (Totenbeschaubefund), Sterbeurkunde from the Standesamt and the Leichenpass or Überführungsbewilligung, issued by the district authority (Bezirkshauptmannschaft) or, in statutory cities, by the Magistrat.
  • Switzerland: medical death certificate (ärztliche Todesbescheinigung / certificat médical de décès), extract from the death register (Auszug aus dem Todesregister / acte de décès) from the Zivilstandsamt or état civil, and the cantonal Leichenpass (laissez-passer mortuaire). As Switzerland is outside the EU, customs formalities apply on exit.

Italy, Spain, France, the Netherlands and Belgium

All five apply CIEC Convention No. 16, so their multilingual extracts need no apostille for Serbia.

  • Italy: certificato necroscopico (medical death certificate) and estratto dell'atto di morte from the Comune, which also authorises the transport; crossing the border requires the passaporto mortuario, issued as a rule by the prefettura and in some regions by the Comune.
  • Spain: certificado médico de causa de la muerte, certificado de defunción from the Registro Civil, certificado de embalsamamiento (embalming or temporary conservation) and the salvoconducto mortuorio. The application goes to the local health authority, and exit is authorised by Sanidad Exterior in the province of death; a violent death also needs a court's permission.
  • France: certificat de décès, acte de décès from the mairie, the mayor's authorisation to close the coffin (autorisation de fermeture du cercueil) and the prefecture's authorisation to transport the body abroad, granted in the département where the coffin is closed, plus the passport of the deceased. A violent or unclear death first needs the prosecutor's consent.
  • Netherlands: verklaring van overlijden (doctor's statement), akte van overlijden from the municipal civil registry (Burgerlijke Stand), the verlof tot begraven of cremeren and the laissez-passer for transport abroad, both from the municipality of death. The municipality needs a doctor's confirmation of natural death or the prosecutor's consent first.
  • Belgium: certificat de décès / overlijdensattest, acte de décès / akte van overlijden from the municipality, as a rule a certificat de non-contagion confirming the death was not caused by an infectious disease, and the laissez-passer mortuaire from the competent Belgian authority. A violent or unclear death requires the prosecutor's consent before the body is released.

Sweden, Denmark and Norway

None of the three applies CIEC Convention No. 16, so every document intended for Serbian authorities is apostilled and translated.

  • Sweden: dödsbevis (death certificate), intyg om dödsorsak (cause-of-death certificate) and an extract from the population register kept by the Swedish Tax Agency (Skatteverket).
  • Denmark: dødsattest, an extract from the civil register (CPR) and the ligpas, the permit to take a coffin across the border. An urn needs no ligpas, but the Serbian documents remain the same.
  • Norway: legeerklæring om dødsfall (doctor's declaration of death), dødsattest, a smitteattest confirming that the death was not caused by an infectious disease, and police permission (tillatelse fra politiet) to take the coffin out of the country. Norway is outside the EU, so customs formalities apply on exit.

Croatia, Slovenia, Montenegro and Greece

Of these four countries, only Greece needs an apostille; Croatia, Slovenia and Montenegro apply the CIEC convention. Note also that Croatia calls its own exit permit (posmrtni putni list) a sprovodnica too — it lets the remains leave Croatia, while the Serbian sprovodnica lets them enter Serbia.

  • Croatia: potvrda o smrti (coroner's certificate), izvadak iz matice umrlih on the international form, accepted in Serbia without legalisation, and the Croatian sprovodnica (posmrtni putni list) — Croatia's own exit permit, issued by the sanitary inspector responsible for the place of death. The application names the border crossing and the registration number of the funeral vehicle.
  • Slovenia: the coroner's certificate (potrdilo mrliškega oglednika), the extract from the civil register on the multilingual form and the posmrtni potni list, which the Health Inspectorate of the Republic of Slovenia issues at the funeral company's request. The cause of death is given in Slovene with its international disease classification code.
  • Montenegro: medical death certificate, the multilingual extract from the register of deaths, the transport permit of the competent Montenegrin authorities and the passport or ID card. Montenegro is not in the EU, so there are customs formalities at the border with Serbia.
  • Greece: medical certificate of death (ιατρικό πιστοποιητικό θανάτου), the death record from the municipal registry (ληξιαρχική πράξη θανάτου), a permit to transport the remains abroad and the passport or ID card. Because Greece never ratified CIEC Convention No. 16, the documents need an apostille and translation.

The Serbian permit (sprovodnica): what the mission asks for

The sprovodnica is Serbia's permit to bring human remains into the country. It is issued by the Serbian embassy or consulate responsible for the deceased's last place of residence, on application by a family member or an authorised funeral company. The missions generally ask for the following, in the original:

  1. the passport of the deceased
  2. a request stating the place of burial and the transport route
  3. the death certificate issued by the foreign authority (for example a Sterbeurkunde or Auszug aus dem Todesregister)
  4. the medical report on the cause of death (for example a Todesbescheinigung)
  5. the transport permit of the country of death (for example a Leichenpass)
  6. for foreign nationals, confirmation that a grave site in Serbia has been secured

Bosnia, Montenegro, North Macedonia, transit and urns

The sprovodnica covers Serbia only. For a funeral in Bosnia and Herzegovina, the embassy or consulate of Bosnia and Herzegovina issues a laissez-passer for the transfer of remains — what families often call the “sprovodnica for BiH” — and the form attached depends on whether the burial is in the Federation of BiH, Republika Srpska or Brčko District. Montenegro and North Macedonia work in a similar way, through their own missions. When the route to the place of burial crosses Serbia, a Serbian transit permit is needed as well.

Repatriation of ashes follows the same principle: an urn brought into Serbia also needs a sprovodnica, with the cremation certificate added to the usual documents, and the urn must be sealed and protected for the journey. Some missions ask for further confirmations, so we always check the list with the one responsible for the case.
